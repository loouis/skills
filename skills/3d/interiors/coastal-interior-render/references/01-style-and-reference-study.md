# Style and reference study

## The visual contract

Warmth comes from related timber, mineral and textile colours, gentle reflected light and tactile surfaces. Restraint comes from proportion, negative space, limited material families and one clear focal point. Neither means everything should be beige, matte or evenly bright.

Build a material hierarchy before objects: quiet light wall; warm oak joinery; slightly different flooring; honed pale stone with one controlled mineral gesture; soft flax sofa against a deeper greige rug; tan or tobacco seating; small muted metal highlights. Use a terracotta, olive or blue-grey accent only when it improves the whole composition. In the developed example, a smaller terracotta cushion worked better than the oversized green one. That is an established preference for this style, not a universal prohibition on green.

## What the surveyed references contribute

Historical study: ten Gold Coast/Dubai developments, 157 source files reviewed through contact sheets, with selected full-size images. Files included repeated scenes, amenities and exterior imagery; this was not an exhaustive census or 157 unique apartment interiors. See the official source links in [REFERENCES.md](../REFERENCES.md). Construction status and availability are deliberately omitted here because they change.

| Reference | Observed visual lesson | Application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burly Residences | Pale timber, substantial stone, sculptural edges, muted warm metal, shadow beneath overhangs, deeper living-room accents | Primary warmth/material hierarchy; retain recess shadows |
| MIRA | Long calm stone plane, recessed support, timber niche, directional daylight, tightly related finishes | Restraint, construction depth and texture instead of many decorative objects |
| COAST | Darker framing, broad bands of light, large floor modules against smaller vertical tiles and reeded glass | Distinct texture scales and framing depth |
| Royale | Built-project imagery with balcony joints, deep slab shade and ocean glare | Reality check for details and brightness; do not assume every image is a photograph without attribution |
| Enclave | Strong veined island against quieter surroundings, olive and bronze accents | One statement surface, not several competing stones |
| Ocean House | Repeated curves, warm timber, display glass, rounded furnishings | A consistent family of shapes; avoid copying all decorative lighting |
| ORLA | Large glazed volume, low furniture, sparse dark tables, negative space | Proportion and outlook as luxury; double height is not a default apartment dimension |
| Six Senses | Darker vertical timber, thick pale stone, bronze and fine-legged seating | Preserve grain and local highlights in darker finishes |
| Armani Beach Residences | Graphic openings, fins and light patterns in reviewed architecture/amenity images | Architecture can supply identity; this set did not establish an apartment-kitchen recipe |
| The Alba | Distinct interior schemes, layered mineral/timber materials and sculptural forms | Pick one coherent scheme before borrowing details |

Published source facts are narrower than visual impressions. The historical MIRA page described honed travertine, European oak flooring, timber-look laminate joinery and brushed nickel. A timber appearance does not prove solid timber. Burly collection pages named different stones in different collections; do not present one collection's stone as the specification for every kitchen.

## Reference acquisition and comparison

1. Prefer official developer, architect, designer and manufacturer sources. Search additional sources when requested or when current product/specification facts are needed.
2. Record source page, creator if stated, date, rights, image dimensions and file hash. Save originals unchanged in the receiving project, not in a public skill package.
3. Label each claim `observed`, `published` or `proposed`. Do not infer exact lens, BRDF, lighting rig, construction status or AI/CGI provenance from visual appearance alone. Find credits or state that the method is unverified.
4. Make a survey sheet for variety, then inspect originals individually. Choose a kitchen, living, material, upholstery and exterior benchmark. Include built photography where available.
5. Compare similar framing at equal display scale. A tiny reference beside a huge noisy crop is not a fair comparison. Inspect original detail separately after the matched comparison.
6. Write the three most consequential differences before touching the scene: for example, weak recess shadows, uniform veneer and sofa tailoring. Do not translate every difference into a new object.

## Composition and room identity

Choose one primary subject: kitchen, living room, view or detail. Give it a clear silhouette and supporting contrast. Test the thumbnail before adding props. Place foreground, middle ground and distant view where useful; retain the room's believable size.

The kitchen should feel assembled and usable. The living room should offer conversation, a comfortable view/TV relationship and routes around occupied furniture. A dining area should not disappear just because the hero camera favours the island. The balcony should feel like another usable room.

For bedrooms, transfer the material/light hierarchy but rebuild the functional brief: bed access, storage, curtains, bedside reach, mattress/duvet thickness and compressed pillows. For bathrooms, design tile modules, corners, drainage, screen thickness, mirror contents and wet transitions. These extensions are principles, not a previously validated bedroom or bathroom scene.

## Avoid the generic luxury trap

Do not add several statement stones, oversized foliage, decorative lights, shiny gold and patterned textiles simultaneously. Avoid a uniform orange grade, cyan shadows, bloom on every edge or an exterior exposed identically to the room. Richness should survive with decorative props hidden: construction depth, restrained variation, fabric weight and a coherent light field do most of the work.
